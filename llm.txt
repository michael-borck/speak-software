# Preface 

## Why This Book Exists

Somewhere in a shared drive right now is software that runs a real business, written by someone who cannot read a line of code. A booking page. An invoice checker. A dashboard the boss looks at every morning. It was built by describing what was wanted, in plain language, to an AI agent. And it works.

The software industry does not know what to do with this. Neither do the schools. The old answer was: learn to code, for years, then build. The new answer from the hype machine is: stop learning, just vibe. Both are wrong, and the gap between them is where this book lives.

Programming has moved up an abstraction level. That has happened before (machine code to assembly, assembly to languages, languages to frameworks), and every time, two things happened. More people could build. And the durable skills moved up with it: not the syntax, but the thinking underneath. Specification. Decomposition. Verification. Taste. This book teaches those skills at the newest layer: you describe, an agent builds, and you are the one who decides whether it is true.

We call the bad version of this vibe coding: prompting until something looks right, never asking why. This book is not against it. Vibe coding is a legitimate tool for prototypes with a small blast radius, and pretending otherwise is gatekeeping. What this book is against is unmanaged vibe coding: shipping a black box to real users, with real data, and no way to know when it breaks. Boris Cherny, who built Claude Code at Anthropic, put the industry's best current answer simply: prototypes can be black boxes, but production code written by AI should be held to a higher standard than human code. And that standard is met with guardrails, not heroics. The developer's role is no longer the builder. It is the mini CEO of a department of one.

## The Department of One

You have been handed an engineering department: brilliant, tireless, overconfident, and it forgets everything you do not write down. One agent is an intern. A folder of well-written instructions turns it into a specialist. A filing cabinet of those folders turns it into a department. Twelve laboratories (each a real client brief, each killing one misconception) teach you to run it.

The companion labs are free and live at michael-borck.github.io/programming-labs. This book is the narrative and the harness: the why behind each lab, the guardrails the labs leave you to build, and the management layer that turns twelve exercises into a working practice. Three follow-on staff-tool labs turn this book's harness chapters into tools your department keeps: the slop gate, the evidence-trio linter, and the environment pre-flight.

## Who This Book Is For

- Business-minded people who will never write code by hand (and should not have to)
- Students and career changers who want to build real things now
- Developers' colleagues and managers who need to evaluate AI-built software
- Programmers, too: the management layer here is the one your job is becoming

## What This Book Is Not

It is not a Python book. You will not learn syntax; you will learn to specify so precisely that syntax stops being the bottleneck. (Code Python, Consult AI is the book for the language itself.)

It is not a prompt cookbook. Prompts appear throughout, but a prompt without a specification, a verification plan, and a decision log is delegation with extra steps.

It is not an argument that AI always gets it right. A third of this book is about the opposite: AI output is a hypothesis, and the guarded pipeline, not your confidence, is the verdict.

And it is not Converse Python, Partner AI, its companion on the shelf. That book teaches you to programme alongside AI, line by considered line. This one assumes you will never touch the code at all, and teaches you to direct anyway. If you cannot decide between them: if you want a language under your fingers, choose the companion; if you want a department at your command, choose this.

## If You Are Feeling Uncertain

You do not need to be "technical". That word is a gatekeeper, and the gate it guards just moved. If you can write a clear email, you can write a clear specification. If you can organise a folder, you can organise a department. The people who appear to have mastered AI tools are weeks ahead, not years. This book starts where you are.

## How This Book Is Structured

Part I: The Department of One: the frame, the slop question, and the first spec. Part II: The Guardrails: checklists that run, version control as a flight recorder, context that persists, evidence of done, and the assembled slop defence. Part III: Judgement: taste, the last 20%, the filing cabinet of skills, and slop in the supply chain. Part IV: The Mini CEO: decomposition, audits, shipping, and the staff of sub-agents you will one day manage.

Every chapter pairs a lab from the companion series with the reasoning the lab leaves implicit, and closes by building one piece of the harness you will rely on for everything after.

# Hello, Department of One 

> You are not learning to code. You are learning to run the people who do, and there is only one of them.

Lab companion: Lab 1, Hello, Department of One. This chapter is the reasoning; the lab is the doing.

## The Concept First

Imagine being handed an engineering department. One employee. Brilliant: ships in minutes what used to take weeks. Tireless: never bored, never tired, never offended. And overconfident in a way no human employee has ever been: it will announce, in fluent confident prose, that the thing it just made works, when it does not.

You are the manager. That is the entire frame of this book, and everything strange about the AI era becomes ordinary once you accept it. The skill of the next decade is not writing code. It is running a department of one brilliant intern: briefing it, checking its work, keeping the records straight, and knowing when it is bluffing.

Managers do not need to type the code. Managers do need four things, and you will build all four in this chapter:

1. A place where the work lives: a repository ("repo"), the department's filing cabinet
2. A way to brief the employee: a prompt, then a specification
3. A way to check the work: running it, then testing it
4. A record of decisions: so the department remembers, and so you can prove what happened

## Understanding Through Real Life

### The Intern You Already Know

Think of the best intern you have ever worked with. Fast. Eager. Produces impressive-looking first drafts in minutes. Now stack the exaggerations: this intern has read everything ever published, works in seconds, costs pennies, never sleeps, and has zero judgement about your business, no memory beyond this conversation, and no shame about inventing facts.

Would you give that intern a task with no instructions? Of course not: you would be paying to generate rework. Would you accept their work without looking at it? Never: not because they are dishonest, but because confidence and correctness are different qualities, and the intern has only the first one.

That gap, between confidence and correctness, is where every chapter of this book lives.

### Why a Repository, Not a Chat

Your first instinct will be to work in the chat window: ask, copy the answer, paste it somewhere, lose it. Everyone starts there. The chat is where you talk; the repository is where you keep. A repository is a folder with a perfect memory: every version of every file, every change, every reason, recorded forever, undoable at any moment.

The single habit this chapter installs: if it is not in the repository, it does not exist. Not the code only: the brief, the decisions, the complaints, the version you rejected. A department that keeps no records is a department you cannot manage.

## Discovering the Department with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: The Vague Request

Open your AI agent (the labs use a command-line agent; any equivalent works) and type exactly:

Make a website

Watch what comes back. The agent guesses, and its guesses are competent, generic, and not yours. Note the assumptions it made: what the site is for, who visits it, what "make" even means. You have just met the department's central limitation: it does not ask. It assumes.

### Exploration 2: The Briefed Request

Now try again, with a brief:

Make a single web page for a cafe called Sunrise. It shows the name,
the tagline "Fresh coffee, warm welcome", and three menu items with
prices. One file. No frameworks.

The result is smaller, closer, and checkable, because the brief gave the department something to be measured against. Keep both attempts. The gap between them is the skill you are here to build.

### Exploration 3: Give It a Memory

In your project folder, create a file named `AGENTS.md` and write your brief into it:

bash
# 1. Make the folder a repository — the filing cabinet
git init

# 2. Let the department work, briefed
#    (your agent writes index.html from AGENTS.md)

# 3. Record what was done — who, what, when, why
git add index.html
git commit -m "Add cafe landing page from brief"

Three commands. `git init` opens the filing cabinet. The agent writes. `git add` and `git commit` file the result with a note. That is the whole ritual this chapter asks you to adopt: work, then file, with a message that says what happened.

"Update" tells a future reader nothing. "Add cafe landing page from brief" tells them what happened and hints at why. When the audit comes (and Chapter 15 is an entire audit), the commit messages are the department's testimony.

## Business Connection

A department of one changes the economics of small software. A page that would have cost a week of a contractor's time takes minutes of briefing. But the economics only work if the work is kept: the businesses drowning in AI-era messes are the ones whose "developer" worked in chat windows, with no repo, no records, and no way to fix what eventually broke.

The repo is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between owning your software and renting it from a conversation you can no longer find.

## Practice Exercises

Exercise 1.1 (Level 1). Initialise a repository and commit one file the agent produced, with a message that says what it is.

Exercise 1.2 (Level 2). Redo the cafe brief with a different business (a plumber, a tutor). Keep the same "done" list; change only the details. Notice what the agent got right without being told.

Exercise 1.3 (Level 3). Break the brief on purpose: remove one line from "done means". Compare what the agent delivers with and without it.

Exercise 1.4 (Level 4). A colleague says repositories are unnecessary because "the chat history has everything." Write the strongest case against, using one real consequence.

Exercise 1.5 (Level 5). Draft the AGENTS.md for a business you actually care about. Keep it. Chapter 6 turns it into a proper context folder.

## Chapter Summary

- Your agent is a department of one: brilliant, tireless, overconfident
- The repository is the department's memory: work is not done until it is filed
- Briefs beat requests: the agent is measured against what you wrote, not what you meant
- Commit messages are management records, for the audit you cannot yet imagine

## Reflection

Before moving on, ensure you:

- [ ] Have a repository with at least one committed file
- [ ] Wrote an AGENTS.md the agent actually reads
- [ ] Can explain the confidence/correctness gap in one sentence

## Your Learning Journal

1. What surprised you about the vague-versus-briefed difference?
2. What would your department's ideal briefing contain?
3. What did the agent assume that you did not write down?

The department works (for the demo). In the next chapter, we test the demo's honesty: why "it works" is the beginning of the sentence, and how the illusion is built.

# The Slop Question 

> The dispute is not restrained versus vibe. It is: what is the blast radius?

Lab companion: Lab 2, The Demo Illusion. Read this chapter first; the lab is where the feeling becomes a habit.

## The Email That Named It

Somewhere inside Anthropic, someone asked a question that thousands of developers were asking quietly, at their desks, with growing unease: "What to do about slop?"

The question went to Boris Cherny, the creator of Claude Code. The developer who wrote it described two camps forming in the wild. One camp works the way developers always worked: AI as an accelerator, but every line reviewed, explainable, and maintainable by a human (the restrained approach). The other camp treats generated code as a black box: run it, check the output, move on. Never read it, never understand it. The permissive approach. Vibe coding, taken to its conclusion.

The worry underneath the question was sharper than either workflow. Vibe-coded software, left unmanaged, degenerates into a tangle nobody can maintain, "slop." And developers feel caught: reviewing everything is becoming impossible at the volume AI produces, but not reviewing feels like the road to being the person who shipped the mess.

Cherny's answer was not to pick a side. It was to dissolve the dilemma, and the way he dissolved it is this chapter.

## Match the Method to the Mission

The mistake in the slop debate is treating "should I read the code?" as a single question with a single answer. It is not one question. It is two:

1. What is the blast radius? If this software breaks (silently, at the worst moment), what gets hurt? A birthday-party invitation list? Nobody. A payroll run? Everyone.
2. How long will it live? Thrown away tonight, or on its second year of service?

From those two answers, the rule falls out:

- Low stakes, short life. Prototypes, one-off scripts, experiments you will discard: treat the code as a black box. Check the output, not the code. Vibe coding is the correct tool here, and gatekeeping it is posturing.
- High stakes or long life. Anything real users touch, anything that stores data, anything someone else depends on: the code gets reviewed, tested, and explained. No black boxes.

And then the twist that surprises people: for that second category, Cherny's bar for AI-written code is higher than for human-written code. Not equal. Higher. A human colleague's code arrives with context: you saw the conversation, you know their intentions. AI code arrives with none of that. It must prove itself entirely through checks and tests, because there is no implied understanding to fall back on.

The blast radius of a change is everything it can break: users affected, data touched, systems depended on, and, since software outlives intentions, every future reader who has to maintain it. A script only you run, on data you own, that you can delete in ten seconds, has a tiny blast radius. The same script wired to your customers has a large one. Radius, not language and not framework, decides how much process you owe.

## Vibe Coding, Properly Understood

Notice what this is not. It is not the series arguing that vibe coding was a mistake, and it is worth being honest that earlier books in this collection sound stricter than this chapter. The resolution is scope.

Vibe coding is a legitimate technique at the layer where mistakes are cheap. Andrej Karpathy, who coined the term, meant something specific: give in to the flow, let the model drive, accept that you are not really reviewing. For a weekend prototype, that is fine. For anything that ships, the same behaviour (accept, run, move on) is how slop is manufactured.

The difference between the vibe coder and the intentional prompter is not the tools or even the prompts. It is what happens between the prompts:

- The vibe coder accepts output; the intentional prompter verifies it.
- The vibe coder asks for more; the intentional prompter asks what would break this.
- The vibe coder's sessions start from zero every time; the intentional prompter's understanding compounds.

This book's position, following Cherny: vibe coding is one gear in a car that also has brakes, mirrors, and a licence. The chapters ahead build the brakes.

## The Mini CEO

If the agent writes the code, what is left for you? Cherny's answer: the job changes from builder to manager, the "mini CEO" of a small, fast, literal department.

A mini CEO does not read every line the department produces. That was always a fantasy; at AI volume it is impossible. A mini CEO instead does four things, and this book is organised around them:

1. Sets the standard before the work: specifications, acceptance checks, definitions of done. (Chapters 4, 5.)
2. Builds systems that enforce the standard: automated checks that run without the CEO's mood or attention. (Chapters 6–9.)
3. Judges the results: reading evidence, not code; taste, not typo-hunting. (Chapters 10–12.)
4. Owns the outcomes: accountability, trust policies, and the decision of when the department is out of its depth. (Chapters 14–18.)

Notice what is missing from that list: heroics. When quality drops, the answer is not to read harder; it is to tighten the gate, retrain the department (the context folder), instruct a rewrite, or wait for a better model. We will earn each of those moves.

Delegation is not absolution. When the agent's code leaks data or corrupts files, the accountability is yours: you approved the change, you owned the check that didn't exist. The mini CEO framing is not a way to do less; it is an accurate description of the part that cannot be delegated.

## The Harness Preview

"Automated guardrails" sounds enterprise. It is five small things, and by the end of Part II you will have all of them attached to your own repository:

| Guardrail | What it catches | Built in |
|---|---|---|
| Lint rules | Style drift, obvious errors, unused code | Chapter 9 |
| Tests with a coverage floor | Behaviour that silently breaks | Chapters 5 and 9 |
| TODO/FIXME sweep | The promises the agent made and forgot | Chapter 9 |
| Diff-size budget | The "one small change" that rewrites everything | Chapter 9 |
| Commissioned reviews | The agent auditing its own output on your behalf | Chapters 15 and 18 |

None of these require you to read code like a developer. All of them require you to specify what "good" means, which is the skill this book actually teaches.

Take the last thing you built with AI. Answer the two questions: blast radius, and lifespan. Write the answers down. If you would have treated it as a black box under this chapter's rule, and it has real users, you have found your first slop. That is not an accusation; it is a starting measurement.

## Business Connection

The slop debate sounds like an engineering argument. It is a budgeting argument.

Unmanaged AI code is a loan: speed now, interest later (paid in outages, rewrites, and the day nobody can explain the system your business runs on). Managed AI code is just speed. The guardrails in this book are the loan terms, and they are cheap: an afternoon of setup per project, then minutes per change.

The mini CEO framing is also the honest answer to the question every stakeholder is now asking: "if AI writes the code, what are we paying you for?" You are paying for the person who decides what to build, holds the bar, and can explain the system when it matters. Everything else genuinely is automatable.

Exercise 2.2 (Level 2). Sort five things you have built with AI into black-box / reviewed, using the rule above. Be honest about anything already in the wrong pile.

Exercise 2.3 (Level 3). Cherny says AI-written production code should face a higher bar than human code. Write the strongest counter-argument you can, then write why it fails.

Exercise 2.4 (Level 4). A stakeholder asks: "why are we paying you if the AI writes everything?" Answer in under 100 words without mentioning typing speed.

Exercise 2.5 (Level 5). Design the slop policy for a two-person business: which AI code gets reviewed, by what gate, and who signs. One page. You will revise it in Chapter 9.

2. What was its blast radius, honestly?

# The Demo Illusion 

> "It works" is the beginning of the sentence, not the end.

Lab companion: Lab 2, The Demo Illusion. This chapter is the reasoning; the lab is the doing.

## The Concept First

The agent shows you a working page and says it is done. You look, you click, it works. The illusion is complete. And it was built in seconds, which is what makes it dangerous.

A demo is one path through the software, walked once, by the person who already knows where the stepping stones are. The agent typed your name correctly; it did not type two hundred characters of emoji. It pressed the button in the right order; it did not press it twice, or press back halfway, or lose the network at the wrong moment.

Software is not done when one path works. It is done when the paths you never imagined have been tried. And the only reliable way to try them all is to write them down before the software exists.

## Understanding Through the Coffee Shop

A barista makes your usual perfectly. Is the cafe good? One order proves almost nothing. The cafe is good when the strange orders work: the decaf-oat-extra-hot-half-sweet, the order for here that changes to takeaway, the two customers who arrive at once.

Software is a cafe full of strange orders. The agent built you a signup form, but nobody has yet:

- Submitted it empty
- Submitted it twice, fast
- Typed an email with a doubled `@`
- Typed an address so long it broke the layout
- Pressed submit on a slow train, then again when nothing seemed to happen

Every one of those is a real customer. The demo met none of them.

## Discovering the Illusion with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: The Agent Tries to Break It

The agent built the thing; let the agent attack it. This reversal is the chapter's core move:

You built this signup page for me. Now act as a hostile quality
tester. List every way a real user could break it — strange inputs,
double actions, empty everything. Do not fix anything yet. Just list.

### Exploration 2: Define Done First

Now, before a single fix, write the acceptance list:

Using your hostile list, write an acceptance checklist for this page.
Each item must be a check that can be run and pass or fail — not "the
page should be robust" but "submitting with an empty name shows an
error message and does not send data."

"Should handle bad input" is a wish. "Submitting an empty form shows an error and nothing is sent" is a check. The difference is that a check can be run by someone who was not in the conversation, including a script, including a fresh agent, including you in three months. The whole discipline of this book is turning wishes into checks.

### Exploration 3: The Red, Green Record

Fix nothing until the checklist exists. Then hand the checklist back:

Here is my acceptance checklist. For each item, run it and report
PASS or FAIL with what you observed. Then fix the failing items, and
report each one passing. Do not mark anything PASS that you did not
run.

What you have just done is a miniature of professional software delivery: requirements agreed before the build, verification after, nothing accepted on vibes. The agent will sometimes report a pass that is not real. Check one at random. Every time.

## From Concept to Code

The record of this chapter is not the fixed page. It is the checklist, filed where the department keeps its records:

Now the page, the checks, and the fixes form one story that anyone (a client, a colleague, a future you) can read without trusting anyone's memory of a chat.

## Business Connection

The demo illusion is where AI-era projects die commercially. A stakeholder sees the demo, assumes the product, and budgets for launch. Then the strange orders arrive (real users, real inputs) and the "working" software costs triple to repair, under deadline, with the credibility already spent.

Defining done before building is the cheapest insurance in software: it costs minutes at briefing time and buys the ability to say, with evidence, "here is what works, here is what does not yet, and here is what it will cost."

## Practice Exercises

Exercise 3.1 (Level 1). Take the agent's signup page. Run the acceptance checklist yourself. Record one PASS and one FAIL.

Exercise 3.2 (Level 2). Add three strange orders of your own to the checklist: orders no one listed.

Exercise 3.3 (Level 3). Ask the agent to break the page on purpose, then to explain each break. Fix them with the agent, one at a time.

Exercise 3.4 (Level 4). A stakeholder says: "the demo worked, why isn't it live?" Write the reply that shows the gap using your checklist: no jargon.

Exercise 3.5 (Level 5). Design "definition of done" for a real project you care about: ten checks, each runnable, none a wish. File it in the repo.

## Chapter Summary

- A demo is one path, walked once, by someone who knew the path
- Strange orders, real users, are what break software
- Done is defined before building: runnable checks, not wishes
- The record of the checks is a first-class document in the repository

## Reflection

Before moving on, ensure you:

- [ ] Watched the agent attack its own work, and found something
- [ ] Wrote an acceptance checklist where every item can pass or fail
- [ ] Fixed from the checklist, not from vibes
- [ ] Committed the checklist itself, not just the fix

1. What did the hostile tester find that you genuinely did not expect?
2. Which of your checklist items was secretly a wish?
3. Where else in your working life are you accepting demos as products?

# Say What You Mean 

> The AI knows what you said. It does not know what you meant.

Lab companion: Lab 3, Say What You Mean, the standalone taster: one agent, one empty folder, one spec.

## The Concept First

When a human contractor takes a brief, they bring a lifetime of unstated context. You say "build a booking page" and they silently supply a thousand decisions: what a booking is, what sensible means, what the culture expects a form to look like. The contractor's filler knowledge hides the gaps in your brief.

The agent has the same filler knowledge about the world, and none about your world. So the gaps in your brief do not get filled with sense. They get filled with the statistical average of the entire internet, which is fluent, plausible, and almost never your business.

A specification is the brief with the gaps closed. Not a genre exercise: a working document whose test is brutally simple. Hand it, alone, to a fresh agent that has never seen your conversation. If what comes back is a product you would accept, you have authored a program in English. The labs call this the Replication Test, and it is the closest thing this book has to a definition of done for thinking.

## Understanding Through the Recipe

A recipe that says "season to taste" assumes a cook. A recipe that says "two teaspoons of salt" assumes nothing. Both produce food; only one survives being handed to a stranger in a strange kitchen.

Specifications are the second kind. The test is not "did the agent understand me?" It is "would a stranger, with only this document, produce the same thing?" If the answer depends on the conversation you had before the document, the document is not the program. The conversation was.

## Discovering Specification with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: Feel the Gap

Ask for something real, deliberately under-specified:

Build me a tip calculator

Then interrogate the result: What currency? Round up or exact? Split bills? What happens with a zero bill? Every answer the agent invented was a gap you owned and did not close. List the gaps. That list is your specification, discovered backwards.

### Exploration 2: Close the Gaps in Order

Now write the specification, but write it as a document the agent must read before doing anything:

Read this specification completely before writing any code.

SPEC — Tip Calculator v1

Purpose: split a restaurant bill N ways with tip.

Inputs:
- bill total (number, currency of the bill, must be > 0)
- number of people (whole number, 1 to 20)
- tip percentage (whole number, 0 to 50, default 20)

Behaviour:
- Each person pays an equal share of (bill + tip).
- Shares are rounded to the nearest cent; rounding residue goes to
  the first person.
- Invalid inputs stop processing with a clear message. No defaults
  for invalid bills or party sizes.

Out of scope for v1: currencies other than the bill's own, tax
calculation, saving history.

Done means: all of the above demonstrated with the five examples
below, plus any case in my hostile list.

### Exploration 3: The Replication Test

Open a completely fresh conversation (no memory of the last one). Paste only the specification, followed by:

Build exactly this. If anything in the spec is ambiguous or
contradictory, stop and ask.

Compare the two builds. Differences between them are the spec's remaining gaps, the places where you were still relying on a shared conversation that a stranger would not have. Close them, and run the test again. When a fresh agent converges on something you would accept, the spec passes.

You did not type the code. You do not need to. You wrote the document that reproduces the product from nothing, and that document, not the code, is what you authored. This is what "programming at the natural-language layer" means, precisely.

## From Concept to Code

The specification lives in the repository, named and versioned like any other file:

From now on, every project in this book carries a `SPEC.md`. When the agent's work is evaluated (by you, by a colleague, by the audit in Chapter 15), the question is never "is this code nice?" It is "does this meet the spec, and would the spec reproduce it?"

Before running the Replication Test, read your own spec looking for the word "it" and any word you would have to gesture at to explain ("nice", "clean", "user-friendly", "handle properly"). Every one is a gap. Replace it with the example that would settle a disagreement.

## Business Connection

The Replication Test is how organisations stop losing knowledge. Any document that only makes sense next to the person who wrote it is a liability with a resignation date. Specifications that pass a stranger test survive staff turnover, vendor changes, and the next model upgrade, because the meaning finally lives in the artefact, not the memory.

This is also the quiet answer to "is prompting a real skill?" Prompting is improvisation. Specification is authorship. The first fades with the chat history; the second is an asset on your books.

## Practice Exercises

# The Checklist It Can't Charm 

> Re-reading is not checking. A checklist that cannot run is a wish.

Lab companion: Lab 4, The Checklist It Can't Charm: one running check; Chapter 9 assembles the full gate around it.

## The Concept First

Last chapter's spec defined done. This chapter makes done checkable. And the difference is the difference between a promise and a mechanism.

When the agent tells you "done!", you have three options: believe it (vibes), re-read the code yourself (a part-time job), or run the definition of done (seconds, and it works even when you are tired, busy, or charmed). Chapter 3's acceptance checklist was a list of sentences. This chapter promotes those sentences into checks: small, runnable, each answering one question about the product with PASS or FAIL.

You do not write the checks. You commission them, from the agent, from your spec. And then you do the one thing this book exists to teach: verify that the checks themselves are honest.

## Understanding Through the Smoke Alarm

Nobody "reads" a smoke alarm. It is a checklist that runs itself, every second, and its entire value is that it fails loudly at 3 a.m. without asking permission. A smoke alarm that required you to remember to look at it would be decoration.

Most people's AI projects have decoration where alarms should be: hopeful notes in a document ("we should validate inputs"), the agent's assurance ("I've handled errors"), a manual test done once, days ago. The slop comes in through exactly that gap.

## Discovering Checks with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: From Spec to Checks

Your spec's "Done means" section converts almost mechanically:

Here is my SPEC.md. Convert every item in "Done means" into a
checklist entry. Each entry must:
- be a single run of something (open this page, run this command,
  enter this input, compare to this expected output)
- state the expected result exactly
- be checkable by someone who has never seen our conversation

Output the checklist only. Do not fix or change the product.

### Exploration 2: Promote the Checks to Code

The most important checks should run without a human reading. For the tip calculator's arithmetic, that means tests (commissioned, again, from the spec):

Write test_checks.py using plain asserts. Each test corresponds to
one "Done means" item or one example from the spec. Include the
rounding-residue rule and the invalid-input rule. Print one clear
line per check: the rule tested and PASS/FAIL. No frameworks.

### Exploration 3: The Charm Test

Now the meta-check, and it is the one that makes this chapter different from a testing tutorial:

Look at the checks you just wrote. Which of them would still pass
if the product were replaced with a hardcoded fake that prints the
expected answers? Rewrite any check that a fake could defeat so it
tests the real behaviour instead.

A check that a hardcoded fake could pass is checking nothing. "Does the page show 24.00?" can be defeated by printing 24.00. "Does the page show the correct split for a 60 bill, 3 people, 10% tip, and for a 61.15 bill, 4 people, 15% tip?" cannot be faked without doing the actual maths. When you commission checks, always ask the fake-detecting question.

## From Concept to Code

The checks file, the kind of thing the agent produces from that prompt:

The agent's checks can be wrong in the agent's favour: too few cases, a fake-defeatable comparison, an expected value copied from the program's output instead of the spec. Audit the checks against the spec exactly as fiercely as you audit the product. Chapter 9 turns this suspicion into a policy.

## Business Connection

A client who can run your acceptance checklist owns the verification themselves. Your honesty stops being the requirement. That is why businesses pay premiums for suppliers who arrive with runnable evidence: it converts trust from a feeling into a procedure. Internally, the same list settles the argument about whether the work is finished before the argument starts.

This is also the seed of the entire quality industry: lint rules, test suites, coverage floors. Chapter 9's full gate is this chapter's checklist with a career. Start the habit small and the scale arrives free.

# The Flight Recorder 

> One wrong move ruins nothing, if everything is recorded.

Lab companion: Lab 5, The Flight Recorder: this chapter is the reasoning; the lab is the doing.

## The Concept First

Aeroplanes carry a flight recorder not because pilots crash often, but because when something goes wrong, the recording is the difference between a mystery and a lesson. Version control is your department's flight recorder. It records every state the software has ever been in, exactly who put it there, and exactly how to get back.

What that buys a manager who never reads code is fearlessness. The single biggest behavioural difference between people who ship software and people who tinker is not knowledge: it is whether making a change feels dangerous. With a flight recorder, no change is dangerous. Every experiment is undoable in one command. The agent can be told "try the ambitious version" without risking the working version, because the working version is recorded, permanently.

## Understanding Through the Undo Keys

Everything you already use has this feature. Email has "undo send". Documents have version history. Photos have a recently-deleted album. The undo is not a luxury; it is what makes you use the tool boldly.

The department's version control has three moves that matter, and you already understand all of them from everyday software:

1. Commit: save a named snapshot ("works with receipt printing", "the version I showed the client")
2. Branch: try an idea on a parallel copy while the main version stays safe
3. Revert: return to any recorded snapshot, exactly

Everything else (merges, rebases, cherry-picks) is plumbing for teams. A department of one needs the three moves and the discipline behind them.

## Discovering the Recorder with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: The Fearless Ask

In a repository with one committed working version, make the ambitious request:

The current page works. On a new branch named "bold-redesign",
rebuild the layout with a completely different structure. Do not
touch the main version. When you finish, show me both.

Then look at both. Keep the redesign, or switch back with one command:

text
History A:
  a1b2c3d update
  e4f5a6b update
  b7c8d9e final version

History B:
  f1e2d3c Add menu section with three items
  a9b8c7d Fix price alignment in menu
  6f5e4d3 Add address block to footer

Ask your agent which history it would rather investigate a complaint from. Then ask it to explain what makes History B usable. The answer will be: each commit is one idea, and each message says what happened. That is a management standard, not a technical one, which is why you are allowed to have an opinion about it.

### Exploration 3: Rehearse the Disaster

The recorder only earns trust when you have used it in a fire drill:

I want to rehearse a recovery. On the current page, make a change
that breaks something obvious (as if a bad edit landed). Commit it
as "attempt new header". Then revert the repository to the previous
commit and confirm the page works again. Show me each command.

Run this drill until the sentence "we can always go back" is something you believe, because you have personally done it.

## From Concept to Code

The chapter's habit, as a rhythm rather than a command set:

If you can describe the change in one sentence, commit it in one sentence. If you cannot describe it in one sentence, the change is too big: split it. This policy, kept honestly, is the entire art of version control at department-of-one scale.

## Business Connection

The flight recorder is why small teams can out-manoeuvre big ones. A team with perfect version records can attempt three designs in an afternoon, show all three to the client, and lose nothing on the two that fail. A team without records must argue in meetings because trying is expensive. Clients do not buy "we were careful"; they buy "here are the three options we built you this week."

And when a supplier asks for your money back, the flight recorder is the evidence: here is what was delivered, when, by which change. Chapter 15 turns that evidence into an audit.

- Commit often, one idea per commit, honest messages

- The history is management evidence, and Chapter 15 will read it

# Teach the Intern 

> Every chat that starts from zero wastes everything you already learned.

Lab companion: Lab 6, Teach the Intern: this chapter is the reasoning; the lab is the doing.

## The Concept First

Your department has one crippling trait left: amnesia. Every fresh conversation, the agent arrives knowing everything about software and nothing about your project: your standards, your decisions, the client's quirks, the three things you decided were out of scope last Tuesday. So you re-explain. And the re-explanations drift, because explanations given casually are never quite the same twice.

The fix is embarrassingly physical: a folder of markdown files in the repository. Instructions the agent reads at the start of every session. Decisions recorded as they are made. Preferences the agent should stop guessing about. This is the context folder (with `AGENTS.md` as its front door), and it is the single highest-leverage artefact in AI-era work.

Two reasons it matters more than any clever prompt. First, it converts your private knowledge into a company asset: the department stops depending on your presence. Second, it is the foundation of the skill-folder architecture in Chapter 12: the filing cabinet that lets one agent become a whole department. You cannot organise skills you have not first written down.

## Understanding Through the New Barista

A new barista asks the regulars their orders every morning for a month. Sweet, but slow. And the day the barista is replaced, all that learning walks out. The good cafe writes the orders down.

Your context folder is the orders notebook. The test of every entry: could a replacement worker do it right from the file, without asking? If a note only makes sense to you, it is a memory aid. If it works for a stranger, it is a system.

## Discovering the Context Folder with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: Audit the Re-explanations

Think back over your last three agent conversations. What did you explain more than once?

Here are the last three briefs I gave you in separate conversations.
Identify everything I re-explained — every instruction, preference,
or decision that appeared in more than one brief. List them as
candidates for a standing instructions file.

That list is your context folder's first draft. Repetition is the signal: anything explained twice belongs in a file.

### Exploration 2: The Front Door

The file `AGENTS.md` at the repository root is read by agents before they start work. Fill it with what a replacement needs:

text
project/
├── AGENTS.md          # Front door: standards, decisions, never-list
├── SPEC.md            # What is being built and what done means
├── DECISIONS.md       # The decision log: what, why, when
├── CRITIQUE.md        # What you would still change, and why
└── content.md         # The client's actual words and prices

The discipline that keeps it alive: the moment a decision is made, it goes in the file. Decisions recorded later are decisions reconstructed from a chat transcript, which is archaeology, not management.

An agent reading "always ask before deleting" in one file and "clean up old files automatically" in another will pick one and run with it, silently. When the department does something inexplicable, the first place to look is a contradiction between standing documents. One topic, one file, one ruling.

## Business Connection

The context folder is the difference between your AI advantage and an AI advantage the business owns. Everything in your head is a single point of failure with a notice period. Everything in the folder survives your holiday, your resignation, and the migration to next year's model.

It is also the cheapest onboarding ever invented. A new employee, a new contractor, a new agent version: all of them start by reading the same four files. The business that writes its decisions down is the business that can change hands.

# Trust, Then Verify 

> "It sounds right" is the most expensive sentence in AI-assisted work.

Lab companion: Lab 7, Trust, Then Verify. This chapter is the reasoning; the lab is the doing.

## The Concept First

The agent's explanations are fluent, structured, and confident. That fluency is the product working as designed, and it is also the attack surface. An answer can be well-organised, appropriately detailed, and entirely wrong, in the same paragraph.

So the rule: trust is fine; trust is not verification. You may absolutely accept the agent's account of what it did (provisionally, as a hypothesis). Verification is the separate act of making reality confirm it. The professional workflow is literally the chapter title: trust first, because trusting is cheap; verify always, because confidence is not evidence.

## Understanding Through the Wikipedia Footnote

A Wikipedia article reads confidently and is mostly right. Nobody serious disputes it because it is confident: they click the footnote. The footnote is the difference between "I read a claim" and "I checked a source."

The agent's explanations need footnotes, and in software the footnote is always the same thing: run it. Not "look at the code and see if it seems right": run the thing on the inputs that matter and observe what comes out. Chapter 5 built the checks; this chapter is about the habit of demanding they actually ran, on evidence you can see.

## Discovering Verification with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: Demand the Receipts

After any build, the weakest response is "looks good." The strong pattern is to request evidence in a fixed format:

For every item in the acceptance checklist, show me:
1. The exact command or action you performed
2. The exact output observed
3. PASS or FAIL against the checklist item

Do not summarise. Do not mark anything PASS that you did not
actually run in this conversation.

### Exploration 2: The Spot-Check

Then verify one at random (genuinely, yourself, in a terminal or browser). You are not checking whether the agent can do the work; you are calibrating its reliability. If the spot-checked item fails while the receipts claim PASS, you have learned something about this agent, this task type, or this session that no policy could have taught you.

An agent can explain a bug fix fluently and incorrectly. The explanation is generated the same way the code is: predicted from patterns, one plausible word at a time. Fluency and accuracy are different axes. The only footnote that counts is the observed output of a run you witnessed.

### Exploration 3: The VET Reflex

Conversation, Not Delegation compresses the verification habit into three letters. Apply all three to anything the department produces:

- V (Verify): run it on a known input; compare to the expected output from the spec
- E (Explain): can you say how it works, in your own words, without reading the code first? If not, you do not own it yet
- T (Test): poke an edge case the agent did not mention. Change one thing. Does it fail loudly or silently?

The reflex takes minutes. Skipping it is how a plausible function becomes a production incident with your name on the approval.

VET is the per-deliverable habit. Above it sits a per-use decision: how much checking does this kind of work deserve at all? Prove, Don't Persuade, the companion volume on verification for applied AI, ships the Trust Tool for exactly that: place the intended use on a green, yellow, red grid by exactness and consequence, then check accordingly. A private prototype sits green and needs the receipts only; anything a customer depends on sits yellow or red and needs the verification witnessed, not just claimed.

## From Concept to Code

The verification habit leaves a record, like everything else in this book. Chapter 3's acceptance file gains a column (observed, with date) and the spec's hostile list becomes the source of edge cases to poke by hand:

The `NOT tested` row is the most valuable line in the file. It is an honest list of what you do not know, which is exactly what the next work session should start with.

## Business Connection

Verification is the product you sell when you sell "AI-era services". A business does not pay you to prompt; it pays you to be the person whose sign-off means the strange orders were tested. The receipts file (commands, outputs, dates) is how that sign-off survives being questioned in a meeting six weeks later.

The same file is your defence against the agent's own confidence. When a stakeholder asks "but the AI said it worked," the answer is: "and I verified it, here is the run." That sentence is the entire professional identity this book is building.

Exercise 8.1 (Level 1). Demand receipts for your next build in the exact three-part format. Spot-check one at random.

Exercise 8.2 (Level 2). Apply the VET reflex to something you accepted yesterday. Run it, explain it, poke it. Record what changed in your assessment.

- Trust the fluency; verify the claim; the two are different axes
- Receipts: exact command, exact output, PASS/FAIL; no summaries
- Spot-check one claimed pass at random, every time

# The Slop Defence 

> You cannot read every line. You can refuse to ship without a gate.

Lab companion: Lab 4, The Checklist It Can't Charm: that lab builds one running check; this chapter assembles the whole gate around it. Lab 13, Close the Gate then ships the assembled gate as a tool your repos keep, with the reference implementation in this book's `tools/slop_check.py`.

## The Gate, Not the Heroics

The slop question resolved into a rule: production code written by AI gets a higher bar, enforced by systems rather than stamina. This chapter builds that system. By the end, every change to your repository will pass through the same five gates, in the same order, whether you made the change or the agent did:

| Gate | Tool | Catches |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Style and obvious errors | `ruff check` | Dead code, unused imports, rookie mistakes |
| 2. Behaviour | `pytest` | Anything that stopped working |
| 3. Coverage floor | `pytest --cov-fail-under` | Code no test has ever touched |
| 4. Forgotten promises | TODO/FIXME sweep | The agent's "temporary" shortcuts |
| 5. Change-size budget | diff line count | The silent full-rewrite |

None of these requires you to read code. All of them require you to have decided what "good" means, which you did, in the spec.

## Commission, Don't Hand-Write

For the rest of the book, the pattern is the same: you describe the gate; the agent builds it. Paste this prompt into your agent, in your repository:

Build a quality gate for this repository as a single script named
slop_check.py that I can run before any commit. It must:

1. Run ruff check on all Python files and fail on any error.
2. Run pytest and fail on any test failure.
3. Enforce a coverage floor: fail if test coverage of src/ is below 80%.
4. Count TODO and FIXME occurrences in tracked files and fail if the
   count exceeds 5.
5. Show the count of changed lines versus the main branch, and fail if
   the diff exceeds 400 lines (my change-size budget).
6. Print one line per gate: PASS or FAIL with the reason.

Keep the script under 100 lines. Use only the Python standard library
plus ruff and pytest, which are already installed. Add a __main__ guard
so it can be executed with python slop_check.py.

It names the five gates, the failure conditions, the output format, and the constraints (the same structure as every good spec: task, requirements, done). If the result is missing a gate or over the line limit, that is not a reason to settle; it is a reason to push back, precisely.

## The Kit, in Full

Because you can read it (and in this book, you always can), here is the gate the agent should produce, essentially:

text
Add a pre-commit hook that runs python slop_check.py before every
commit and blocks the commit if it exits non-zero. Use the pre-commit
framework with a local hook.

And for the strongest version (the gate that runs even when someone bypasses their own machine), commission the same five gates as a GitHub Actions workflow. You will not write the YAML. You will describe the gate in a sentence and let the agent produce the workflow file, then verify it by opening a pull request from a branch and watching it run. The verification habit matters more than the YAML, which nobody reads anyway.

The numbers (80% coverage, 5 TODOs, 400 changed lines) are starting points, not laws. What is non-negotiable is that the gate exists and fails loudly. Tighten thresholds when the department earns it; never widen them silently to let one change through. Widening the gate to pass a change is the exact move this book exists to prevent. And if it must be done, it is done in a commit that says so.

## Business Connection

Every gate is a translation of a business sentence. "Customers should not find the bugs" is a coverage floor. "We should not forget our promises" is a TODO sweep. "Nobody rewrites the product in secret" is a diff budget. When a client asks what your higher standard actually is, you do not gesture at care and attention. You show them the five gates and the runs that failed.

# The Taste Test 

> "Best" is not objective. That is precisely why they need you.

Lab companion: Lab 8, The Taste Test. This chapter is the reasoning; the lab is the doing.

## The Concept First

Ask the agent for the same feature twice and you will get two working implementations. Both pass the checks. Both satisfy the spec. Neither is wrong. And choosing between them is the first task in this book that the agent cannot do for you: not because it lacks ability, but because "better" here is not a fact. It is a position.

This is taste: the judgement that one good option fits your situation better than another good option. The agent can generate options endlessly and enumerate trade-offs fluently. It cannot know that your client phones support at 11 p.m., that your successor will be a temp, or that you would rather debug a boring file than a clever one. Those preferences, held and defended, are the deliverable.

So this chapter promotes critique from a reaction ("I like this one") to an artefact: CRITIQUE.md, a filed document of positions with reasons. The rule that makes it honest: every entry must be arguable. A statement is arguable if a reasonable person could disagree: "I chose the version with the explicit loop because a temp will maintain this and explicit beats clever" is arguable. "The first one is better" is not.

## Understanding Through the Menu

Two excellent restaurants offer you the same ingredient. One serves it in a thirty-second dish that celebrates the produce raw. One braises it for six hours. Asking which restaurant is "best" is a category error: they have made different arguments about what the ingredient is for.

The agent's two implementations are the two restaurants. Your job is not to score them like a test. Your job is to work out what each one is arguing (optimising for speed? for clarity? for the smallest change?) and then decide which argument your situation is actually making. A feature needed for tomorrow's demo and a feature that will run for five years deserve different winners.

## Discovering Taste with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: Generate the Dilemma

Ask the agent for two deliberately different solutions to the same small problem:

Write a function that reports the largest number in a list, in two
versions: one optimised for a beginner to read, one optimised for
the shortest possible code. Do not tell me which is better. Show
both, complete.

### Exploration 2: Build the Vocabulary

The difference between "I prefer this" and a critique is vocabulary. Ask for the axes:

Compare the two versions across these dimensions, one sentence each:
how obvious it is to a non-programmer reading it; what it does with
an empty list; what it does with a non-number smuggled into the list;
and what it costs to add a second rule (ignore negatives) later.

Now you have something arguable. Dimensions (obviousness, edge behaviour, cost of change) are how taste gets taught, defended, and handed to the next person.

### Exploration 3: Take a Position

Write CRITIQUE.md. Positions, with reasons:

bash
git add CRITIQUE.md
git commit -m "Critique: keep explicit version; revisit if maintenance changes"

And the habit generalises. From now on, every fork the agent offers you gets the same four questions:

1. What is each version optimising for?
2. Who will maintain it, and what do they already know?
3. What does each do when the input is strange?
4. What does it cost to change later?

Four answers, one position, one reason. That is a taste test.

You can ask the agent to enumerate dimensions, play advocate, or pressure-test your position: all good uses. Do not ask it "which is better?" and accept the answer. Its preference is the average of the internet's; your situation is the specific thing that makes this decision yours. Delegate the menu. Keep the choosing.

## Business Connection

Taste is the part of the work that does not commoditise: the recurring argument of this series, arriving here in its most concrete form. Two businesses with the same AI can produce equally working software; they cannot produce equally chosen software. The choice records who you are: the firm that prefers boring and maintainable, the studio that ships fast and rewrites, the consultancy that documents everything.

That is also the client-facing answer to "how is this different from my nephew with ChatGPT?" The nephew generates. You generate, then take responsibility for a position, in writing, with reasons that survive your absence. The difference is the CRITIQUE.md.

## Practice Exercises

Exercise 10.1 (Level 1). Generate the two largest-number implementations. Write the four-question comparison from memory first, then with the agent.

Exercise 10.2 (Level 2). Ask the agent to defend the version you rejected. Write down whether it changed your mind, and if so, which argument did it.

Exercise 10.3 (Level 3). Take any real thing you built earlier in this book. Write a CRITIQUE.md of your own decision to build it the way you did: position, named situation, admitted cost, reversal condition.

Exercise 10.4 (Level 4). Have the agent produce three implementations instead of two. Does a third option change your position? If not, what does that tell you about the strength of your criteria?

Exercise 10.5 (Level 5). Write your taste charter: the five standing preferences that will guide your choices in every project: named, with one-line reasons. File it; expect to edit it, not obey it.

## Chapter Summary

- Same request, two good answers: choosing between them is judgement, not lookup
- Taste needs vocabulary: dimensions like obviousness, edge behaviour, cost of change
- CRITIQUE.md makes taste a deliverable: arguable positions, not verdicts
- Delegate the menu; keep the choosing

- [ ] Admitted what your choice gave up
- [ ] Filed the critique in the repository

# The Last 20% 

> The first 80% is a demo. The last 20% is the product.

Lab companion: Lab 9, The Last 20%. This chapter is the reasoning; the lab is the doing.

## The Concept First

The agent is superb at the first 80%. Given a clear spec it produces the working feature, the reasonable layout, the passing demo, fast. What it does not do, reliably, is finish. The last 20% is everything the spec did not force: the empty state nobody designed, the error message that sounds like a machine, the page that works but feels unloved, the one weird input the checklist never imagined.

This is not a complaint about the agent. The last 20% is hard for the same reason taste is hard: it requires knowing what the thing is for. The agent can complete requirements; only you can see the gap between "meets the spec" and "feels finished." Your job in this chapter is to see that gap, name it, and direct the department to close it: item by item, without accidentally rebuilding the product.

## Understanding Through the House Viewing

Nobody buys a house because the frame passed inspection. The frame is essential, but what sells is the door that closes softly, the light switch where your hand expects it, the absence of the smell. Those things were all "finishing touches" to the builder and deal-breakers to you.

AI-built software has excellent frames. The finishing is where it shows: the button that works but has no pressed state, the form that works but forgets everything on error, the page that loads but has no opinion about loading. Each one is invisible on the demo. Each one is the reason a visitor quietly decides the site is not real.

## Discovering the Last 20% with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: The Fresh-Eyes Pass

You are the wrong judge: you know what everything is supposed to do. Find the right judge, or simulate one:

Act as a first-time visitor who has never seen this project and is
mildly impatient. Walk through it and list every moment where you
hesitated, wondered, or noticed something rough. Do not fix
anything. Order the list by how much each moment would affect
whether a visitor stayed.

### Exploration 2: Name the Categories

The fresh-eyes list is always the same families, which makes it directable:

- Empty states: what does the page look like with no data yet?
- Error moments: what does the user see and feel when something fails?
- Boundaries: the longest name, the zero items, the slow connection
- Loading: what happens in the seconds you forgot to design?
- Consistency: three pages, three button styles?
- Words: machine messages ("Error 500") where human ones belong

Commission the agent to walk each family deliberately, one prompt per family, collecting candidates. The quality jump between "make it nicer" and this list is the entire lesson.

### Exploration 3: Direct Without Rebuilding

Now the directing skill, the hard part. Vague polish requests ("make it feel more professional") trigger rewrites. Item-by-item requests preserve the product:

Three changes, nothing else:
1. When the item list is empty, show the message "Nothing here yet —
   add your first item above." — not any other text.
2. The submit button gets a pressed visual state.
3. All error messages are rewritten as full sentences with a period.

Everything else stays exactly as it is. Show me a diff of what you
changed.

The diff request is the safety harness: you review the change, not the whole product, so the last 20% cannot quietly become a new first 80%.

## From Concept to Code

Polish items are tracked like everything else, as commitments, not vibes:

And when an item is genuinely a decision rather than a task (the wording of an error message, the colour of a warning), that is CRITIQUE.md material, not a chore. The last 20% is where taste from Chapter 10 does its daily work.

The last 20% is never done. Decide how much of it a given product deserves (a throw-away prototype deserves almost none, and that is correct) and stop on purpose. Stopping is a decision to record in DECISIONS.md, like any other.

## Business Connection

The last 20% is the entire visible difference between "built with AI" and "built by a professional." Anyone can produce the working 80% now; the market has noticed. What clients cannot produce themselves is the judgement of which rough edges matter, the discipline of item-by-item direction, and the restraint to stop.

Charge for it honestly. "Polish" is not an euphemism for padding; it is a named phase with a list, a diff review per item, and a stop decision. Projects that skip it look cheaper and are more expensive, because the visitor who hesitated at the ugly empty state did not send an email explaining why they left.

## Practice Exercises

Exercise 11.1 (Level 1). Run the fresh-eyes pass on any project from this book. List five rough moments, ranked by visitor impact.

Exercise 11.2 (Level 2). Pick the empty-state family. Commission all its fixes in one item-by-item prompt. Review the diff.

Exercise 11.3 (Level 3). Find one place where polish direction did trigger a rebuild. Diagnose which word in your request caused it.

Exercise 11.4 (Level 4). A prototype must ship tomorrow. Write the DECISIONS.md entry: which last-20% items are consciously abandoned, and why that is correct here.

Exercise 11.5 (Level 5). Build the "polish walk" prompt-set: six prompts (one per family) reusable on any project. File it in your context folder permanently.

## Chapter Summary

- The agent finishes requirements; finishing the product is directed judgement
- Rough moments come in families: empty states, errors, boundaries, loading, consistency, words
- Item-by-item direction with a diff review: never "make it nicer"
- Stopping on purpose is a decision to record, not a failure to admit

- [ ] Walked your own project as an impatient first-time visitor
- [ ] Commissioned fixes by item, with a diff review
- [ ] Registered the remaining gaps as tracked items, not guilt
- [ ] Made (and recorded) a conscious stop decision

2. What does your product's empty state say about how much you expect people to use it?
3. Where in your work do you confuse "done" with "demoed"?

# The Filing Cabinet 

> The most sophisticated AI orchestration is a well-organised folder.

Lab companion: Lab 6, Teach the Intern built your first context folder; this chapter scales it into an architecture.

## The Concept First

Every organisation adopting AI runs into the same obstacle. It is not model quality and it is not cost. It is the context wall: the challenge of getting your organisation's instructions, data, and tools into the model's narrow working memory at the right moment, without overwhelming it or watching it improvise what it did not know.

The industry's default answer is infrastructure: a separate bespoke agent for every task, wired together in a framework, each with its own setup and maintenance. For a department of one, that is a mistake of scale: seven fragile contraptions to do the work of one good agent and a filing cabinet.

The alternative (the one Anthropic productised as Agent Skills, and the one this book has been inching toward since Chapter 7) is almost embarrassingly physical:

- One capable agent that can read, write, and run tools
- A folder per skill, named for the workflow it serves
- Inside each: a markdown instruction file (the skill's rules and steps, a `SKILL.md`) alongside any data and scripts the workflow needs

When work arrives, the agent is pointed at the folder. It reads the instructions, runs the scripts, and, effectively, becomes the specialist the folder describes. One agent, many skills, zero infrastructure. Spin up as many conversations as you have hands: each one is a specialist, instantiated on the fly from a document you wrote once.

## Understanding Through the Filing Cabinet

You do not hire a different accountant for every form the business files. You hire one competent accountant and give them a filing cabinet: this drawer for tax, with the checklist taped inside the drawer; that drawer for payroll. The accountant reads the right procedure when the work arrives.

An agentic framework is ten tiny robots, each hard-wired to one form, plus the wiring between them. The filing cabinet is one professional plus an organised cabinet. When a new form appears, the framework needs a new robot; the cabinet needs a new piece of paper.

That is the whole architecture. And its deepest property is the one that matters to a business: the cabinet survives the accountant. Skills written down are assets. Skills kept in an agent's memory, or in one person's prompting habits, are favours that expire.

## Discovering Skills with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: Feel the Context Wall

Ask the agent to run a repeatable workflow you never wrote down (say, your monthly expense routine, from memory, in one prompt). Watch what it gets wrong or invents. Every invented step is a file that does not exist yet. The context wall is not the model's memory limit; it is the paperwork you never wrote.

### Exploration 2: Build the First Skill Folder

Turn your most repeatable workflow into a folder:

project/
└── skills/
    └── weekly-expenses/
        ├── SKILL.md        # the procedure
        └── totals.py       # the tool, if the workflow needs one

The `SKILL.md` is a brief to a stranger:

markdown
# Skill: 

## When to use        <- so the agent picks the right skill
## Inputs             <- exactly what must be at hand first
## Procedure          <- numbered, specific, in order
## Never              <- the mistakes you have already paid for
## Done means         <- the checkable definition, imported from Ch 5

Two rules keep the cabinet healthy. One skill, one folder: a SKILL.md that covers "expenses and invoicing" is two skills and will be read wrong. And scripts earn their place: if a step needs precision (totalling, date maths, file renaming), put it in a small script the skill calls. Precision belongs in code, judgement belongs in the markdown.

Skills drift. The procedure that was right in September fails in December because the receipt format changed. Once a quarter (or whenever the agent does something inexplicable), read the whole cabinet top to bottom and fix what no longer matches reality. Chapter 15's audit is this discipline, practised on someone else's cabinet first.

## Business Connection

This architecture is how the department of one becomes a department that serves the whole business. Every repeatable workflow in the organisation (invoicing, onboarding, reporting, the weekly order) becomes a skill folder: procedure in markdown, precision in scripts, the never-list written in blood. Staff point the agent at a folder; the specialist appears; the work is done the company's way, not the model's way.

The strategic property is independence. The cabinet is plain files: they work with this year's agent and next year's, they move between tools, and they are readable by any human who wants to audit what the AI is actually doing. That is the approach.

Because the cabinet is plain files, the brand of agent matters less than the sales page suggests. Evaluate any agent with three questions and ignore the feature list.

First, where do the standing instructions live? A file you can open, version, and share beats a text field on a settings page. Second, what can the agent reach: a project folder, the whole file system, the web, or nothing at all? Third, what does the tool conceal: the exact prompt it sent, the files it read, the model version that answered? Chapter 8's rule applies here too. Anything you cannot inspect stays unverified until you check it another way.

## Practice Exercises

Exercise 12.1 (Level 1). Write SKILL.md for a workflow you do weekly. Ten lines. Run the skill; watch what it gets wrong; fix the file, not the chat.

Exercise 12.2 (Level 2). Add a `## Never` section from real experience: one entry per mistake that has already cost you something.

Exercise 12.3 (Level 3). Move one step of the skill from prose into a small script. Compare reliability before and after, over three runs.

Exercise 12.4 (Level 4). A colleague must run your skill while you are away. Watch them do it. Every question they ask is a missing line in SKILL.md.

Exercise 12.5 (Level 5). Design the cabinet for your whole role: the folder tree, one line per skill, marked built/needed. This is the blueprint Chapter 14 runs.

## Chapter Summary

- The context wall is unwritten paperwork, not a technology limit
- One capable agent plus a cabinet of skill folders beats a framework of fragile contraptions
- SKILL.md holds the judgement; scripts hold the precision; the never-list holds your scars
- Skills are assets: they survive staff changes, agent changes, and time

## Reflection

Before moving on, ensure you:

- [ ] Built one skill folder and ran it successfully
- [ ] Wrote a never-list from real experience
- [ ] Moved precision steps into scripts
- [ ] Watched a fresh conversation become a specialist from a folder

1. What workflow surprised you by being skill-able?

3. If you left tomorrow, which parts of your role have no folder yet?

# Slop in the Supply Chain 

> The agent does not just write code. It chooses what to trust.

Lab companion: Lab 7, Trust, Then Verify. Same reflex, new target: not the agent's code, but everything the agent pulls in to make it work.

## The Concept First

When the agent builds a feature, it rarely builds everything. It reaches for the department's supply chain: existing packages, libraries, and snippets that already do parts of the job. That reach is what makes it fast, and it is also the widest unsupervised door in your project.

Two slops live in that door. Hallucinated dependencies: the agent confidently instructs you to install a package that does not exist, or worse, did not exist until scammers noticed that AI assistants keep recommending the same made-up names and registered them, filled with malware. Security researchers have a name for this: slopsquatting. The fake package waits, squatting on the exact name the agent is most likely to invent. And unvetted real dependencies: packages that exist, work, and come with licences you have not read, maintenance that stopped years ago, or maintainers you are now trusting with your data.

The agent treats the supply chain as a menu of names it remembers. You are the only one at the table who can check the kitchen.

## Understanding Through the Price Tag

A package is never free. Every dependency is a hire: it can do the job, but it can also resign (abandoned), change personality (a breaking update), leak your information (poorly maintained), or have been an impersonator all along (the squatting attack). Businesses learned this lesson the hard way with human-open-source years ago; AI multiplies the pace of hiring past the pace of checking.

The discipline is the same one any purchasing officer uses: nothing enters the building without three questions answered. What is this? Who stands behind it? What does it cost us beyond the price?

## Discovering the Supply Chain with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: The Bill of Materials

Ask the department to declare what it used:

List every package, library, CDN script, and external snippet the
current project depends on. For each: its name, where it came from,
what it does for us, and what we lose if we removed it.

The list is your bill of materials, and its length is a finding in itself. Projects built by agents accumulate dependencies the way drawers accumulate cables.

### Exploration 2: The Vetting Pass

Now interrogate each hire:

For each dependency on that list, answer:
1. Who maintains it, and when was the last release?
2. Is the name spelled exactly as the real project spells it? Check
   character by character — report anything that looks like a
   misspelling of a better-known package.
3. What licence does it use, and does that licence permit commercial
   use?
4. If we deleted it, what would we write ourselves, and how many
   lines would that be?

Slopsquatting works because the fake name is nearly right: a swapped letter, a reordered word. The defence is unglamorous: before installing anything the agent suggested, look the package up in the official registry by typing or searching the name yourself, and confirm the project exists, is maintained, and is the one you meant. The agent's spelling is not evidence.

### Exploration 3: The Snippet Question

Dependencies are not the only imports. Ask:

Which parts of this project did you copy from documentation,
examples, or training memory rather than writing fresh? For each,
tell me where it came from and whether it carries a licence.

AI-copied snippets usually carry no licence at all, which is not automatically a problem, but is definitely something a business should know it is shipping.

## From Concept to Code

The vetting answers belong in the repository, where Chapter 7's context folder lives:

The table is the asset. When a dependency breaks next year (and one will), you will know instantly which hires you made, and whether the fix is an update, a replacement, or a 20-line rewrite.

Independent of supply chains, three categories of information never enter an AI prompt: credentials and API keys; personal data about identifiable people; and anything your organisation, client, or regulator has marked confidential. Redact first, then paste. No workflow in this book needs those categories, and no blast-radius argument rescues them.

## Business Connection

Supply-chain vetting is where the mini CEO does risk management that a manager from 2019 would recognise instantly: approved-supplier lists, purchase records, spot audits. The vocabulary changed (registries instead of vendors), and the pace increased, but the control is the same, and businesses already have muscle memory for it.

The competitive angle: businesses that adopt AI without a dependency policy are accumulating invisible liabilities. The business that can hand an auditor a bill of materials with vetting dates is safer, saleable, insurable, and eligible for work the others are quietly being disqualified from.

## Practice Exercises

Exercise 13.1 (Level 1). Produce your project's bill of materials. Count them. Would you have guessed that number?

Exercise 13.2 (Level 2). Run the character-by-character name check on every dependency. Confirm each in the official registry yourself.

Exercise 13.3 (Level 3). Find the licence of each dependency and write one sentence: what this licence allows, forbids, and obligates.

Exercise 13.4 (Level 4). Remove one dependency and replace it with the smallest code that does its job. Record whether the rewrite was more or less than the lines you saved.

Exercise 13.5 (Level 5). Write your dependency policy into your context folder: vetting steps, approval rules, review cadence. Have the agent read it back and challenge a loophole.

## Chapter Summary

- The agent hires from a menu it remembers; you check the kitchen
- Slopsquatting: fake packages registered on the names agents invent; verify exact names in the official registry, yourself
- Every dependency is a hire with a licence and a maintenance future
- The bill of materials is the asset: vetted hires, dated, in the repository

- [ ] Produced and vetted a complete bill of materials
- [ ] Confirmed at least one package name in the official registry, yourself
- [ ] Wrote the never-paste list into your context folder
- [ ] Started a dependency table with vetting dates

# Run the Department 

> Big things are not built in one go. They are decomposed, dispatched, and verified.

Lab companion: Lab 10, Run the Department: this chapter is the reasoning; the lab is the doing.

## The Concept First

Everything so far has been one product, one conversation, one gate. Real work arrives as a fog: "we need the whole ordering thing." The mini CEO's first management system turns fog into sequence, and the tool is not a framework. It is decomposition, written down as issues, dispatched to the department one at a time, each landing on verified ground.

The principle the chapter defends: the agent builds features; you build the sequence. A big prompt that asks for everything produces the confident average of a big thing: plausible, interlocking, and wrong in ways that only surface when users arrive. Ten small briefs, each with a check, each landing on the last, produce a system.

## Understanding Through the Renovation

Nobody renovates a house by telling the builder "renovate it." The work is decomposed: roof before painting, wiring before walls, kitchen last. Each stage is inspected before the next begins, because the next stage builds on the inspection, not on the hope.

Software sequences the same way, and the order is rarely obvious to clients. Clients describe destinations; the department needs staging posts. Translating destination-talk into staged work, and then resisting the urge to run stages in parallel, is the management skill this chapter installs.

## Discovering Decomposition with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: The Fog Meeting

Brief the agent with the fog itself:

A client said: "We need the whole ordering thing — customers order,
we see the orders, and we know what's happening." I do not know what
stages this should be built in. Interview me one question at a time
until you can propose a staged build: each stage small, independently
checkable, and useful on its own. Do not propose anything yet.

The interview is the point. Watch the agent ask about payment, notifications, and scale while you discover you had not thought about refunds. The questions are the management.

### Exploration 2: The Issue List

The interview becomes a decomposition, and the format matters. Each stage is an issue: small enough to brief fully, useful enough to verify alone:

bash
git add ISSUES.md
git commit -m "Decompose ordering project into 5 staged issues"

And each landing is recorded against its issue:

Every time the agent proposes something different from the spec, and you choose, you are writing the project's real history. Stage notes that record deviations honestly are worth more than the code they describe. They are how the next stage's brief gets better.

## Business Connection

Issue-decomposition is how you can now sell staged certainty. The client hears: "Stage 1 in a week, checkable, useful alone; we decide together whether stage 2 starts." Compare that to the classic software quote: months of work, all or nothing, trust me. Decomposition converts trust into checkpoints, and checkpoints are what let small businesses take on projects far above their size.

It is also the defence against the agent's one great weakness at scale. Left alone, the agent will attempt the whole fog in one confident act, and the result is the plausible-but-broken architecture from Chapter 4's grid (the danger zone, precisely). Decomposition is how a mini CEO keeps every unit of work in the sweet spot: small, precise, verified.

## Practice Exercises

Exercise 14.1 (Level 1). Run the fog interview on any vague request you have received recently. Count the assumptions it exposed.

Exercise 14.2 (Level 2). Write a five-stage issue list for the same request. Each stage: one sentence of scope and one runnable check.

Exercise 14.3 (Level 3). Land stage 1 end to end: dispatch, verify, record, commit. Note where you were tempted to dispatch two stages at once.

Exercise 14.4 (Level 4). Take a real project of yours that stalled. Decompose it into stages so small that the first could land today.

Exercise 14.5 (Level 5). A client insists all stages ship at once. Write the argument for staging in their terms: money, risk, and what they can show their board at each checkpoint.

## Chapter Summary

- The agent builds features; you build the sequence
- Decompose into issues: small, independently checkable, useful alone
- One issue per conversation, landed on verified ground before the next
- Recorded deviations are the project's real history, and the next brief's teacher

## Reflection

Before moving on, ensure you:

- [ ] Sat through a fog interview and answered its questions honestly
- [ ] Written an issue list where every stage lands useful
- [ ] Landed at least one stage: dispatched, verified, recorded
- [ ] Resisted parallel, and noticed where the temptation came from

# The Audit 

> The code is not the only record. Sometimes it is not even the true one.

Lab companion: Lab 11, The Audit: this chapter is the reasoning; the lab is the doing.

## The Concept First

Everything this book has taught (specs, gates, flight recorders, context folders) produces a trail: SPEC.md, DECISIONS.md, CRITIQUE.md, the issue list, the commit history. This chapter is where the trail becomes a skill. You audit someone else's department: a provided repository, a colleague's project, or your own work from far enough away to count as someone else.

Auditing is the discipline that proves the rest of the book was not theatre. It is easy to keep good records for yourself. The audit asks: does this trail stand alone? Could a stranger reconstruct what was built, why it was built that way, and whether the product matches the story? For the reader, this is also the exact skill of evaluating vendors, contractors, and, increasingly, AI agents you did not personally brief.

## Understanding Through the House Inspection

A house inspection is not a house tour. The tour shows you the kitchen. The inspection asks for the permits, checks the wiring against code, walks the roof, and reports what the paint is covering.

An audit is the same posture applied to a software trail. The README is the paint. The questions are underneath: does the spec match what was built? Do the decisions in the log match the commits in the history? Does the acceptance checklist actually run green, and was it possible to fake?

## Discovering the Audit with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: Read the Trail Before the Product

The amateur opens the code. The auditor reads the trail first, in order:

You are auditing this repository. I have not shown you the code yet.
Read SPEC.md, DECISIONS.md, CRITIQUE.md, and the commit history.
Then answer, from the documents alone:
1. What was this supposed to be?
2. What decisions were made, and do the reasons given still hold
   together?
3. Where would you expect the risks to be?

A trail that answers those three questions is doing its job. A trail that cannot is the finding. The code has not even been opened yet.

### Exploration 2: The Match Test

Now the product enters, and the audit becomes adversarial:

Here is the actual product. Compare it to the trail:
1. Which acceptance checks from SPEC.md does it genuinely pass? Run
   each one; do not accept claims.
2. Which parts of the finished product are NOT explained by the spec
   or the decision log?
3. Which parts of the spec were quietly dropped?

The two most productive questions in auditing are the last two: unexplained product (features that arrived from nowhere) and quietly dropped promises (spec items that faded). Both are slop signatures.

### Exploration 3: The Verification Audit

Finally, audit the verification itself, the receipts from Chapter 8:

Review the acceptance checklist and its recorded results. For each
claimed PASS, assess: could this check have been faked by a hardcoded
answer? Was the expected output copied from the spec or from the
program's actual behaviour? List every check you would re-run
yourself, and re-run them.

## From Concept to Code

The audit produces a report, and the report has a genre. Findings first, ordered by consequence; evidence attached; and the discipline that separates an audit from a review (observations, not verdicts about people):

The fastest way to internalise this chapter: run the same audit on your own current project and imagine the auditor dislikes you. Whatever makes you wince (the missing decision entry, the unexplained feature, the check that claims more than it ran) is the thing to fix before a real audit finds it. Chapter 16 asks for exactly that wince.

# Ship Something True 

> You do not need permission to ship. You need evidence it is true.

Lab companion: Lab 12, Ship Something True, the capstone: the full loop, owned.

## The Concept First

Everything in this book converges on one moment: the change goes from your repository to a real person who was not in the conversation. Shipping is not the opposite of care. It is care's proof. A product that sits in a finished state, fully checked and never delivered, has passed no test that matters, because the only users who count are the ones who did not read your spec.

"True" is the word the labs chose deliberately. Not working: working is Chapter 3's illusion, passed by one path walked once. True means: does what the spec says, survived the strange orders, passed the gate, carries a trail that explains it, and is in the hands of someone it was built for. That is the whole pipeline, landing.

## Understanding Through the Restaurant Opening

Restaurants do not open with the full menu. They open small: a soft launch, a short menu, the dishes the kitchen can execute blind. The opening is not the end of construction. It is the first time construction meets reality, and reality orders something unusual, at volume, at 8:15 p.m. on a Saturday.

Shipping software is a soft launch. The gate has run; the receipts exist; and reality will still find the thing you did not imagine, because reality always does. The mini CEO ships expecting that, watches for it, and treats the first strange order from a real user as the most valuable data the project has ever collected.

## Discovering Shipping with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: The Landing Choice

Where true things live depends on what they are, and the choice is yours to make deliberately:

My project is [describe: what it does, who uses it, how often, what
breakage costs]. Recommend where it should live — a static host, a
shared drive, a managed platform, or a server I control — with the
trade-offs in my terms: cost, effort, blast radius, and what happens
when it breaks at 3 a.m.

### Exploration 2: The Pre-Flight

The gate has run, but shipping adds questions the gate does not know:

I am about to make this project public for real users. Audit it for
pre-flight: What should be true before launch that is not checked
yet? What happens on day one, day thirty, and day 365? What is the
rollback plan if a user reports a disaster?

Before shipping, write the sentence: "if this goes wrong, I will ___ and the users will see ___." If the blank takes more than a minute to fill, ship behind a smaller blast radius first: fewer users, a test group, a copy of the data. The rollback plan is not pessimism. It is what makes day one survivable.

### Exploration 3: Ship, Then Listen

After launch, the department's job changes: watch, log, and resist the urge to react to every report. The first week's reports are a requirements document written by reality. Collect them where the trail lives (DECISIONS.md for what changed, CRITIQUE.md for what you would still change) and sequence the responses through the issue discipline of Chapter 14.

## From Concept to Code

The portfolio value is the point of the whole series: twelve shipped things, each with spec, gate, critique, and launch record. That folder does not say "I know about AI." It says "I run a department." Employers, clients, and funders can see the difference without a word of code.

## Business Connection

Shipping is where the mini CEO's two roles meet. To the department, you are the standard. To the users, you are the supplier. The launch record is the hinge between them: it is the moment the accountability you accepted in Chapter 2's blast-radius exercise becomes real, attached to a URL real people can touch.

And the strategy lands here too. The pipeline you have built (spec, gate, audit, launch, listen) is what lets a department of one ship things that used to need agencies, at a pace agencies cannot match, with a trail that outlives the tooling. That is the new baseline, and you are standing on it.

# The Mini CEO's Staff 

> One agent became a department. A department gets staff.

Lab companion: Labs 10–12, extended. This chapter is the management layer the labs leave for after you can walk.

## The Concept First

Every chapter so far has used one agent, briefed well. The last discipline changes the shape: the mini CEO hires staff: specialised roles the same underlying model plays, each with its own instructions, each checking the others' work.

The reason is the slop question again, at higher volume. One agent building while the same agent self-reviews is a conflict of interest: the reviewer shares the blind spots of the builder. The professional answer is separation of duties: a builder agent working from the spec, a reviewer agent auditing the diff against the spec alone (never having seen the builder's self-assurance), and sometimes a third (a fuzzer, a security pass, a hostile user), each a fresh conversation with no stake in the builder's pride of authorship.

This is Cherny's guardrail stack, staffed. And the staffing plan is written in exactly the material Chapter 12 taught: folders of instructions. The reviewer is a skill folder. The fuzzer is a skill folder. The staff is the filing cabinet, pointed at itself.

## Understanding Through the Newspaper

A newspaper does not let reporters publish directly. Reporter writes; editor checks against standards; fact-checker verifies claims; only then does it ship. Everyone in the chain is a professional; the quality comes from the separation, not from any individual genius. And the editor does not read the reporter's explanation of their own draft. The editor reads the draft.

Your staff works the same way. The builder's conversation is full of context and confidence. The reviewer must arrive knowing only the spec and the diff. Fresh eyes are not a compliment; they are a control.

## Discovering the Staff with Your AI Partner

### Exploration 1: Hire the Reviewer

Write the reviewer's skill folder (once, permanently):

text
(Conversation 1 — builder) Implement stage 3 of the ordering project
per ISSUES.md and AGENTS.md. Commit to a branch when done.

(Conversation 2 — reviewer, fresh) Run the code-reviewer skill on
the diff between main and the change branch.

Then you read the findings and decide, because the CEO signs. Some findings the builder fixes. Some findings you overrule, in writing, in DECISIONS.md, with the reason. That last step is not bureaucracy; it is the difference between having a review process and having a ritual.

### Exploration 3: Plan for Model Change

Staff change. Models change faster. Cherny's advice (when quality drops, "wait for the next model") has a management corollary: the department's output must survive the department being replaced. The way to guarantee that is the pipeline itself: when a new model arrives, re-run the current spec through the Replication Test, re-run the gate, and compare the audit findings to the last model's.

Model upgrade checklist:
1. Re-run the Replication Test on two representative specs
2. Re-run the full gate on the current product
3. Re-audit one landed stage; compare findings to the previous audit
4. Record the model and date in DECISIONS.md — the staff roster changed

The book you are holding is deliberately model-agnostic for this reason. The specs, checks, gates and skills are the durable layer; the model is staff, and staff turn over.

Staff salaries were a line item; model credits are too. Track spend per project the way you track hours. When a task is routine, say so in the brief. Smaller, cheaper models handle routine well, and the frontier model is for the work that earns it. The mini CEO allocates, not just approves. The administrative side (subscriptions, tokens, spend caps, and switching providers without losing work) is the Model Access appendix.

## Business Connection

Staffed AI review is what lets a department of one credibly serve clients larger than itself. The pitch writes itself from this chapter: every change is built by an agent, reviewed by a different agent configured as an adversary, gated by automated checks, and signed by a human who read the findings. That is a control structure some regulated industries would envy. And it runs from a folder.

The staff folder is also the transferable asset at its purest. The reviewer skill you wrote for your project works on the next project unchanged. Your staff follows you, trained, to every job.

## What Does Not Commoditise

Be precise about the claim, because it is the book's last and largest. The pipeline commoditises. Gates get templated; specs get drafted by the department itself; whole workflows fold into skill folders that the agent reads and runs. That is fine. It was always the plan. - Deciding what to build: the judgement of which problem matters

- Taste: the position you take when two good options argue

An agent can generate code into your repository. It cannot decide, on your behalf, what good looks like. When everyone's agent is competent, the competent code stops being an advantage. That is not a consolation prize for people who never learned to program. It is the actual job, arriving ahead of schedule.

# Appendix: The Slop Defence Kit 

The five gates from Chapter 9, as a starting configuration. Tune the thresholds; never remove a gate silently.

| Gate | Tool | Starting threshold |
|---|---|---|

# Appendix: A Slop Lexicon 

- Slop: plausible, unverified AI output that degrades a codebase or body of work.
- Vibe coding: prompting until something works, without reviewing or understanding it. Legitimate where mistakes are cheap.
- Blast radius: everything a failure can hurt (users, data, systems, future maintainers).
- Black-box threshold: the blast radius beyond which code must be read, tested and explained.
- Guardrail: an automated check that fails loudly without human attention.
- Harness: the assembled set of guardrails a change must pass to ship.
- Context wall: the challenge of getting the right instructions, data and tools into the model's working memory at the right moment.
- Skill folder: a folder holding a SKILL.md (procedure) and any scripts/data, read by an agent on demand.
- Sub-agent: a specialist created on the fly by pointing a capable agent at a skill folder.
- Mini CEO: the developer's management role (set the standard, build the gate, judge the results, sign the outcomes).
- Replication Test: a spec, alone, handed to a fresh agent, reproducing a product you would accept.
- Evidence trio (SPEC.md, DECISIONS.md, CRITIQUE.md): the trail an audit can stand on.

# Appendix: Model Access, Credits, and Handover 

The labs' gear guide (RESOURCES.md in the labs repository) covers environments, models, and costs. This appendix covers the administrative layer the labs leave alone: accounts, subscriptions, tokens, and how to switch any of them without losing the department. Principles over prices. Prices move monthly; the principles have not moved in years.

## What you are actually paying for

Three layers, three decisions:

- The agent is the command-line tool that reads and writes files in a folder. Some agents ship with bundled model access; some bring your own key.
- The model access is the intern's brain: a subscription (flat monthly fee) or pay-as-you-go credit consumed per token. A token is the unit models read and you are billed for; the labs' resources list a visualiser that shows your words the way the model sees them.
- GitHub holds the memory. It is free for this work.

## Choosing a payment shape

A subscription wins when you are doing several labs or projects a month: predictable, no meter anxiety. Pay-as-you-go wins at a casual pace (a lab costs roughly a coffee) and for cohorts, where per-student keys with spend caps keep the bill boring. Set the cap before the first session, not after the first surprise.

Free tiers are real but throttled. They suit Lab 3's taster and a first look; mid-arc, the rate limits will cost more patience than money.

Model brokers and free models. Services such as OpenRouter and OpenCode Zen sit between you and many providers: one account, many models, and some free or cheap tiers. Used deliberately, they are a sensible way to run routine work cheaply and to try alternatives without new accounts everywhere. Three cautions:

1. Free models are usually smaller or older. Fine for drafts and routine fixes; do not hand them consequential work (Chapter 17's allocation rule).
2. Read each broker's data policy before pasting anything you would not put on a poster. That caution applies to every hosted model, free or paid.
3. These are examples, not a ranking. The book stays tool-independent on purpose; the named product will age before the method does.

## Local versus frontier

A local model (Ollama and friends) runs on your own machine: free, private, offline. Today's local models are also noticeably weaker interns: more wrong turns, worse tool use, more of your time spent unsticking them. The labs' advice holds: learn to direct on a frontier model, where a strong intern isolates the variable that matters, which is your words. Run a local model later as an experiment, and as a home for routine, privacy-sensitive drafting.

The allocation principle is Chapter 17's: routine work goes to the cheap model, consequential work to the frontier model, and the gate decides whether the cheap model was good enough. The gate does not care what the model cost.

## When you hit a wall

Diagnose first. Three common walls, three different responses:

| Wall | What it feels like | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Rate limit | "Too many requests", resets later | Wait, or slow the pace; nothing is broken |
| Quota or credits | Requests rejected until you top up or the month rolls over | Top up, allocate down, or switch |
| Entitlement | The agent or model is not in your plan at all | Change plan, or move the work to access you have |

None of these blocks the work for long, because the department's memory is the repository, not the chat. Options, in order: wait for the reset; allocate the routine work down to a cheaper model; switch provider using the handover kit below; or pay. Whatever you choose, re-run the gate afterwards. The checks, not the vibe, confirm the new arrangement is fine.

## The handover kit

Models, subscriptions, accounts, and laptops all turn over. The department survives the turnover if everything that matters was written down.

What survives a switch: the repositories on GitHub, the skill folders inside them, the evidence trio, the specs, the gate configuration, the launch records.

What does not: chat history, the agent's memory of your conversations, uncommitted files, and anything installed only on the old machine.

| # | Handover step | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `git status` clean on every project; everything pushed | The memory lives in the repo, not the machine |
| 2 | Skill folders inside the repos, not only in the old tool's memory | The cabinet survives the accountant (Chapter 12) |
| 3 | Evidence trio current; DECISIONS.md records the switch: model, date, reason | The staff roster changed (Chapter 17) |
| 4 | Run the new setup through the pre-flight | Lab 15, The Doctor Is In, and this book's `tools/lab_doctor.py` exist for exactly this |
| 5 | Re-run the gate and one Replication Test on the new model | Parity is checked, never assumed |
| 6 | Retire the old access: revoke keys and tokens, note final spend | An unused key is an unlocked door |

Hand the fresh agent a continuity brief, not a history:

I am continuing an existing project, not starting one.
Read AGENTS.md, SPEC.md, DECISIONS.md and CRITIQUE.md in this
folder before anything else.
The previous model was ; you are . Do not redo
decided work.
Current stage: . Finish by running  and
showing the receipts in the three-part format.

If the Replication Test on the new model reproduces a product you would accept, and the gate passes, the switch is done. Record it in DECISIONS.md and move on.

## How much checking does a switch deserve?

Route by stakes, not by habit. The Trust Tool in Prove, Don't Persuade places an intended use on a green, yellow, red grid by exactness and consequence: a private prototype sits green and needs the gate only; anything a customer depends on sits yellow or red and needs the receipts reviewed before it ships. The VET reflex from Conversation, Not Delegation (Chapter 8) is the per-deliverable habit; the Trust Tool is the per-use decision above it.

# About the Author 

Michael Borck is a software developer and educator working at the intersection of human expertise and artificial intelligence. He teaches applied AI and AI in business at Curtin University, and runs The Human Edge masterclasses through Curtin Executive Education.

Speak Software: Programming at the Natural-Language Layer is for readers who will never write code by hand. It treats programming as a management job: you describe what you want, an AI agent builds it, and your work is specification, verification, decomposition, and taste. The hands-on companion is the Speak Software programming labs, a free set of client-brief exercises at michael-borck.github.io/programming-labs. The labs supply the doing; this book supplies the why, the guardrails, and the harness the labs leave out, including the slop gate, the evidence-trio linter, and the environment pre-flight in this book's `tools/` folder.

His earlier book, Conversation, Not Delegation, argues for treating AI as a thinking partner you converse with. The Python track in this series teaches the language itself for readers who want it under their fingers. Speak Software takes the other interface: no syntax, and a department of one to run instead.

Michael builds educational software and resources, and writes about the 80/20 principle in learning and professional judgement.
